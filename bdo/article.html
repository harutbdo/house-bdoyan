This is the story of one household that became a family name. It begins with a man called Bdo on the high plain of Karin, follows his descendants across a closing frontier in 1829–1830, and ends with a practical guide for every Bdoyan who wants to rebuild the tree, branch by branch, back to him.

I
Chapter I THE FOUNDER

Bdo of the Karin Plain

Բդո՝ Կարնո դաշտից

Every Armenian surname is a small history. Bdoyan — Բդոյան — is the name of one man’s household, carried for more than two centuries across two empires, three modern countries and, today, several continents.

The nameԲդո + -յան = Բդոյան
The Armenian ending -yan (older -ean) means “of” or “belonging to the line of”. Bdoyan therefore simply means “of Bdo’s family”. In village speech the household would also be called by a collective form such as Բդոենք — “Bdo’s people”.

The story begins on the plain of Karin — today’s Erzurum in eastern Turkey. It is a broad, almost treeless upland at close to 1,900 metres, ringed by mountains and drained by the headwaters of the Euphrates. For centuries it was the heart of Bardzr Hayk, “High Armenia”, and its fields were worked from Armenian farming villages. At the start of the nineteenth century there were roughly a hundred of them on the plain alone.

Family tradition places Bdo’s home in one of these villages: Dinarigom (Դինարիգոմ), and the records now bear it out. Samvel Karapetian’s survey of Javakhk, built on the church archives, states that the people of Pokr Khanchali descend from Armenians who moved there from Dinarigom village, Karin district, in 1829–1830. He places Dinarigom on the right bank of the Euphrates, 15 km north of Karin city; its Turkish name today is Myratgeldi. The village also appears, spelled Դինարգոմ (Dinargom), in the 1909 list of the forty-eight Armenian villages of the Karin district printed in the Soviet Armenian Encyclopedia — so the Armenians who stayed behind in 1829 went on living there for another eighty years.

Who was Bdo?

No document yet found carries Bdo’s name, and nothing written in his own hand survives. What we can say comes from the name itself and from how Armenian village society worked.

One thing is almost certain: Bdo was not the name he was baptised with. Armenian priests christened children with the names of saints and of the Bible — Petros, Hakob, Grigor, Baghdasar — and no one was baptised “Bdo”. In daily life, though, villagers called each other by short pet forms ending in -o, and the notes Vard Bdoyan took in Pokr Khanchali are full of them: Papo, Khnko, Gevo for Gevorg. Bdo is one of these.

The likeliest full name is Petros (Peter). Hrachia Acharyan’s great Dictionary of Armenian Personal Names gives Peto (Պետո) and Petik as the usual pet forms of Petros. In Western Armenian speech, the family to which the Karin dialect belongs, Petros is pronounced Bedros — so Peto sounded like Bedo. Written in the Eastern Armenian spelling used in the Caucasus, that sound becomes Բդո: Armenian script does not write the short vowel ə after a first consonant, and the name is said “Bə-do”. A Western Armenian family from Bitlis, the Bedoyans, likewise remember that their surname grew out of Bedrosian.

Baghdasar has also been suggested, but a pet form of Baghdasar would normally keep its gh, which Bdo lacks. Only a register naming one of his sons as “son of Petros” — or of some other name — can settle it, and that is one of the first things to look for in the records described in Chapter VII. Pet names like this were how neighbours actually spoke, and they often outlived the formal name: the man christened Petros lived on as Bdo.

Dating BdoVard Bdoyan was born in 1910, son of Harutyun. At about 28–32 years per generation, counting back five or six steps from Vard places Bdo’s birth somewhere around 1740–1780. His sons and grandsons were the generation that made the journey of 1829–1830.

More telling is that his name, rather than his father’s or his son’s, became the family’s for good. In the villages of the Armenian Highlands a family line was usually called after the head of household whose authority and number of descendants made that house stand out. When the household divided, each branch still answered to that founder’s name. That Bdo became such a name-giver tells us he was the respected tanuter (տանուտեր), the master of the house, of a large and lasting family.

Life in a patriarchal household

Bdo’s family would have lived as a gerdastan (գերդաստան): married sons stayed under their father’s roof with their wives and children, so that three or four generations, often several dozen people, shared one hearth, one store of grain and one set of fields. Authority ran from the eldest man down; daughters married out into other houses, and brides came in.

This is not guesswork borrowed from elsewhere. It is the way of life that Bdo’s own descendant, the ethnographer Vard Bdoyan, spent his career describing (see Chapter IV). He showed that such great households survived across the Armenian Highlands right up to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and that the villages themselves were built in clustered quarters of joined houses — the physical shape of clan loyalty and of communities that had to defend themselves.

A voice from DinarigomIn July 1959 Vard Bdoyan interviewed Simon Davtyan, a man born in Dinarigom who had settled in Leninakan (Gyumri). Simon told him that his father’s household had numbered 33 people, with five cradles rocking in the house at the same time. Vard also described a Dinarigom house with four gomasenyak under one roof.

Dinarigom gives us a living picture of such a house. In 1959 Vard Bdoyan wrote down the memories of a man from that very village whose father’s household had held thirty-three people (see the box). Vard’s description of a Dinarigom house speaks of the same scale: one long building with four gomasenyak (գոմասենյակ) — the raised winter rooms built inside the cattle barn, where the family spent the cold months beside its animals.

On the Karin plain that life meant spring ploughing with heavy wooden ploughs drawn by teams of oxen, wheat and barley harvested in a short summer, flocks taken to the uplands, and long winters when people and animals sheltered in the same half-sunken stone buildings for warmth. It was a hard, close-knit and self-reliant world. In 1828 it was overtaken by war.

Chapter II THE JOURNEY

The Great Exodus, 1829–1830

Կարնեցիների մեծ գաղթը

Between autumn 1829 and 1830, tens of thousands of Armenians left the Karin plain behind. Among them were Bdo’s sons and grandsons.

In numbers 75,000–90,000
Armenians estimated to have left Erzurum province with the Russian army, most of them from Karin itself.

58,000
from Erzurum, Basen, Khnus and Derjan settled in the Akhaltsikhe and Akhalkalaki districts in 1830, by historian A. Melkonyan’s count.

≈ 50
villages founded or rebuilt by them in the Akhalkalaki district alone, where only 1,716 Armenian families had remained in 1829.

In the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–1829 the Russian Caucasus army under General Ivan Paskevich swept into the Ottoman borderlands. Kars fell in the summer of 1828, followed by Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe; in the summer of 1829 the army entered Erzurum itself. For the Armenians of Karin, who had welcomed and supplied the Russian troops, it seemed that Christian rule had arrived to stay.

It had not. By the Treaty of Adrianople, signed on 14 September 1829 (2 September in the Old Style calendar used in Russia), Russia kept the districts of Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe — the region Armenians call Javakhk — but handed Erzurum, Kars and Bayazet back to the Sultan. Fearing reprisals, and encouraged by the Russian authorities who wanted settlers for their new frontier lands, the Armenians of the province chose to leave with the withdrawing army.

The emigration was led by the Armenian prelate of Erzurum, Archbishop Karapet Bagratuni (1779–1856), and it used a right written into the treaty itself: Article 13 allowed the inhabitants of the returned lands to move freely to the other empire. The Ardahan villages left at the end of 1829; the people of Erzurum, Basen and Khnus followed in the summer of 1830. Whole villages packed what they could onto ox-carts — grain, tools, bedding, livestock, the vestments and books of their churches — and moved north-east in columns over the mountain roads toward Kars and the new Russian frontier. Families travelled together and, wherever they could, settled together; a village that left as one community tried to rebuild itself as one community.

For the people of Dinarigom and their neighbours, the road led to the high, half-empty plateau of Javakheti, around the lakes of Khanchali and Paravani. Decades of border warfare had left much of it thinly settled, and the Russian administration directed the newcomers there to farm it. The villages of the Karin plain scattered across that plateau side by side: Dinarigom’s families founded Pokr Khanchali; people from Umudum, which lay at almost the same distance north of the city (16 km), settled Abul and Majadia; those of Hindzk went first to Sazel near Akhaltsikhe and then to Mets Khanchali, across the lake from Pokr Khanchali.

Vard Bdoyan describes how they did it. By old custom, he writes, the Armenians resettled by forming kin quarters: “they acquired not only single houses but whole quarters”, and it was thought unacceptable for a kinsman to build apart from the rest of his clan. A family that had lived as one quarter in Karin rebuilt itself as one quarter in Javakhk.

The move was not just a change of country. Karin and Javakheti lie at almost the same height — about 1,900 metres against 1,950 — with the same short summers, deep snows and open, windswept fields. The migrants brought their ploughs, seed, customs and dialect, and much of that life could be lived again on the new plateau. That continuity is one reason the families of Javakhk kept their Karin identity so strongly, and why the Karin dialect is still spoken there today.

III
Chapter III THE VILLAGE

A New Home on Lake Khanchali

Փոքր Խանչալի · Ջավախք

On the south-western shore of a shallow upland lake, the migrants from Karin founded the village that would be the Bdoyans’ home for the next two centuries: Pokr Khanchali.

Pokr Khanchali today Armenian: Փոքր Խանչալի (“Little Khanchali”)
Georgian: პატარა ხანჩალი (Patara Khanchali)

Pokr Khanchali — “Little Khanchali”, to distinguish it from its larger neighbour Mets Khanchali across the lake — stretches along the southern shore of Lake Khanchali, within 200 metres of the water, on the Javakheti volcanic plateau in today’s Georgia, close to the Armenian border. Its people, Samvel Karapetian writes, descend from the families who came from Dinarigom in 1829–1830, and it has been an Armenian village ever since. For a time it had a few Turkish neighbours as well: a document of 1870 counts eight houses of Tarakama Turks (96 people), there were six in 1884, and in 1918 they left for Turkey.

The Bdoyan quarter

We can see exactly where the Bdoyans lived. In 1950 Vard Bdoyan climbed the rocky hill called Kurch above the village and sketched it house by house, leaving out everything new, so as to record Pokr Khanchali as it had stood in the 1910s. The village was divided into an upper and a lower half, veri mayla and tsatsi mayla, and each half into groups of joined houses, every group the quarter of one or more related families. The old Armenian word for such a quarter, tagh, Vard notes, had already been forgotten in Karin: the settlers called it a mahla, and named it after the eldest of the clan.

The Bdoyans held one block of five adjoining houses at the end of the first group of the lower quarter, beside the Vardanyans and the Yesoyans: the houses of Harutyun, Grigor, Anton and Davit, Mesrop and Zakar Bdoyan. It is the kin quarter in its plainest form — the descendants of one house still living wall to wall eighty years after the migration.

Bdoyents Papo and the first mill

The Bdoyan hay teamHaymaking was done by teams called hampa, formed by kinship and kept almost unchanged for decades: the Ispirents, Avagents, Bdoyents, Davitents, Esoyents and Vardanents teams, among others. Each team cut the first letter of its leader’s surname into its haystacks with a spade, so that Բ 30 meant “Bdoyan, 30 haycocks”.

Vard also preserved the name of the family’s leader in the new village. In the 1830s, he writes, the first water-mill of Pokr Khanchali was built by Bdoyents Papo — “Papo of the Bdoyans” — the nahapet (նահապետ), or patriarch, of the Bdoyans of the village. Water was scarce on the slope of the hill called Atorik where he built it, so it turned only one millstone, but it went on working long after him. When it had come down to Papo’s great-grandsons, the Ayvazyans built a handier mill closer to the village, and a village song teased the Bdoyans about their old one. In 1918 raiders wrecked both mills, and the villagers had to take their grain to the mills of Dilif.

Papo is the earliest Bdoyan known by name. As head of the family in the first years after 1830, he belonged to the generation of Bdo’s sons or grandsons; his great-grandsons were most likely the men of Vard’s sketch.

Counting the village

Every year the diocese asked each parish how many souls it had, so the village can be followed almost year by year. The 78 people of 1853 had become 147, in thirteen houses, by 1866, and 691 by 1914. Then came catastrophe. In 1918 the Ottoman army overran Javakhk; the villagers fled, and hunger and disease followed the refugees. By 1921 only 353 people were left. Anush Bdoyan’s husband, Mesrop Bdoyan — very probably the owner of house 31 — fell fighting in the Dilif pass in May 1918, and his brother Khnko was killed in the village in January 1919. Pokr Khanchali recovered to 593 people by 1926 and to 801, in 215 houses, by 1987. In the Second World War it sent 113 men to the front, and 58 did not come back. The 2014 census counted 554 inhabitants, 99% of them Armenian.

The church of St Hakob

A village of Karin Armenians was organised around its church. Surb Hakob (Սուրբ Հակոբ, St James) is mentioned as early as the 1850s, and in the 1860s it was still a modest wooden building. In 1875 the villagers rebuilt it, with finely dressed stone on the west front and hewn stone on the other walls. It is a hall of 16.8 × 10.3 metres with walls 90 cm thick; inside, two pairs of round columns carry the double-pitched roof. The only door is in the west wall, and the font, cut from a single block of stone, is set into the north wall. A parish school was already working in 1865, with one teacher and ten pupils.

Two inscriptions record the rebuilding. The one on and around the tympanum over the door says, in our translation, that in the days of Catholicos Gevorg the church of Pokr Khanchali “was built and dedicated to Saint Hakob” and blessed by Archbishop Grigor Sakinyan of Akhaltsikhe; its worn last lines include the name of Father Vardan. The second, carved south of the door, says that the church was consecrated “through the efforts and the honest earnings of my dear people” on the feast of the Holy Cross of Varag, 28 September 1875.

The records also name people who served it. Father Vardan Movsisyan was one of its priests from the 1860s to the 1880s — very probably the Father Vardan of the inscription, and the man most likely to have written down the baptisms, weddings and burials of the Bdoyans in those decades. Karapet Khoperyants taught in the parish school in 1875, and at the start of the twentieth century the church held a capital of 257 roubles 69 kopeks in silver. Around the church lies a cemetery with gravestones of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the village also keeps a memorial to the fedayi commander Gevorg Chavush (1870/71–1907).

That church matters to anyone tracing the family. For almost ninety years, from the 1830s to the Soviet period, its priests wrote down every baptism, marriage and burial in the village. Those registers are the most direct written trail from today’s Bdoyans back to the families who arrived in 1830 (see Chapter VII). The gravestones around the church are a second record, carved in stone.

The surname becomes official

The district in numbers1893 — the Akhalkalaki district has 102 villages and about 60,000 people.
1897 — the first Russian census counts 72,709 people in the district; 72% give Armenian as their mother tongue.

In Karin, people were known by their own name, their father’s name and their house. Under Russian rule the authorities began to list every household, its head and his sons in registers, censuses and family lists. Over the course of the nineteenth century the old house-names hardened into fixed surnames written on every document. That is how “Bdo’s people” became, permanently and officially, the Bdoyan family.

The names themselves were older than the records. Summing up Vard Bdoyan’s findings, the editor of his Javakhk book, Rafayel Gharibyan, notes that a kin quarter was usually called after the head of the clan, and that almost every clan that moved to Javakhk kept the name it had borne in Karin before the migration. The Russian clerks wrote down names that the families had brought with them.

The settlers brought their speech with them. The Karin (Karno) dialect (Կարնո բարբառ), once spoken from Kars to Baberd with Erzurum at its centre, survives today mainly in Javakhk — in Akhalkalaki, Ninotsminda and their villages. When older people in Pokr Khanchali speak the village tongue, they are still using the words of the Karin plain that Bdo knew.

Life in Pokr Khanchali followed the rhythm the families had known in Karin: grain and potatoes on the high fields, cattle and sheep, long winters, and the gerdastan household slowly giving way, by the early twentieth century, to smaller families. Into one of those families, in 1910, was born the man who would become the Bdoyans’ best-known son.

The best-known Bdoyan is the ethnographer and folklorist Vard Harutyuni Bdoyan — a son of Pokr Khanchali who spent his life recording exactly the kind of village world his ancestor Bdo had lived in.

Vard Bdoyan was born on 4 March 1910 in Pokr Khanchali, into a farming family. He went to school in the village, finished the pedagogical college in Akhalkalaki in 1932 and taught in secondary schools from 1933 to 1937 before settling in Yerevan, where he completed the Pedagogical Institute by correspondence in 1943.

He never lost touch with home: in 1961 he gave some 300 objects to the regional museum of Akhalkalaki, and he argued in print that Javakhk deserved a museum of its own.

What Vard Bdoyan said

Vard Bdoyan’s ideas speak directly to the Bdoyan story. Here are six of them, drawn from his published work as summarised by the ethnographer Rafik Nahapetyan on his centenary in 2010, and from his Javakhk book.

On ancestors

“There is no people whose later art of dress is not directly connected with the art of its ancestors.”

«Չկա ժողովուրդ, որը տարազի ուշ շրջանի արվեստով անմիջականորեն կապված չլինի իր նախնիների արվեստի հետ»

His conviction that the present is always tied to the ancestors runs through all his work. He traced each region’s costume back to the ancient peoples of the Armenian Highlands and rejected the claim that Armenian dress was simply borrowed from neighbours.

On the great household
The gerdastan lasted for centuries

He traced the patriarchal great family to the rise of the father-centred clan, and showed how it survived through the feudal centuries before breaking up only in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — the very world Bdo’s family lived in.

On kinship words
Native words for “kin”

He held that the words for a blood-kin group, արյունառու (aryunaru) and ցեղ (tsegh), are native Armenian, while ազգ (azg) and տոհմ (tohm) came from Pahlavi and Persian. Through these terms he followed the links between the wider clan and the family line.

On the village
Houses built for kin and defence

The Armenian village of attached quarters and joined houses, which lasted into the 20th century, was in his view the product of two forces: clan tradition on one side and communities defending themselves under hostile rulers on the other.

On hidden memory
Old beliefs survive in disguise

His “theory of disguise” held that ancient beliefs did not vanish with Christianity but lived on in hidden forms. The female-shaped clay salt-cellars of Armenian homes, he argued, still carried the image of the old goddess Anahit, protector of mother and child.

On resettling as kin

“They acquired not only single houses, but whole quarters.”

«ձեռք էին բերում ոչ միայն առանձին տներ, այլև ամբողջական թաղեր»

Writing of the 1830s in Javakhk, he showed that the settlers from Karin rebuilt their villages clan by clan, and that a kinsman who built far from his clan’s quarter was frowned upon. Each quarter was named after the clan’s eldest — which is how “Bdo’s people” could carry his name from the Karin plain to Lake Khanchali.

The book that came out after him

For years it seemed that his most important work for the family would never appear. His archive held about 130 printer’s sheets ready for print, including two large studies of his homeland, The Ethnography of Javakhk and The Folklore of Javakhk; some 20 sheets of the folklore went to the Hayastan publishing house in 1986, the year he died, and were never printed there. Years later an editor handed the folklore manuscript — more than five hundred large pages, saved, as he puts it, by sheer chance from being burned — to the folklorist Rafayel Gharibyan, himself from Javakhk. Gharibyan traced the family and returned it; they entrusted him with the ethnography manuscript as well, and he spent years turning both into one book.

It appeared in 2014 from Yerevan State University Press, with support from the Gulbenkian Foundation: The Ethnography and Folk Art of the Armenians of Javakhk («Ջավախքի հայոց ազգագրությունը և ժողովրդական բանարվեստը»), 752 pages, compiled, introduced and annotated by Gharibyan, with drawings by Vard himself. It is the source of the village sketch, the mill of Bdoyents Papo and the Dinarigom household in this booklet. Vard Bdoyan began collecting at home. His very first recordings, in 1938, were made from an old storyteller and from “his relative” Gevorg Bdoyan of Akhalkalaki; in the summer of 1940 he worked in Pokr Khanchali itself, and among his best storytellers his editor names Varvare, Anush and Balasan Bdoyan. His notes on each of them, with birthplaces and dates, read like the first pages of a family register (see the box).

Varvare Bdoyan, who married Harutyun Bdoyan of Pokr Khanchali in 1887, was a skilled dairywoman and potter who knew many proverbs. Vard was “son of Harutyun” and was born in 1910, and house 28 in his sketch is Harutyun’s, so she was very probably his mother — though the notes never say so, and a register will have to confirm it. Anush lost her husband Mesrop in the fighting of 1918, his brother in 1919 and both her sons, Grigor and Zavik, in 1941–1942; she turned her grief into verses that Vard wrote down and kept with deep respect.

V
Chapter V THE BLOODLINE

One Ancestor, One Family

Մեկ նախահայր, մեկ ընտանիք
THE CORE OF THE FAMILY STORY

Every Bdoyan is a descendant of Bdo. The name itself is the record: Bdoyan means “of Bdo”.

Բոլոր Բդոյանները Բդոյի ժառանգներն են։

Common surnames like Grigoryan or Hakobyan arose independently in hundreds of places, from hundreds of unrelated men named Grigor or Hakob. Bdoyan is different.

  1. The name points to one man.Bdo was a nickname, most likely for Petros. Countless men named Petros left sons who became Petrosyans; only in one known household did the nickname itself harden into the surname. Bdoyan is the name of one house, not a common pattern.
  2. One village, one house.The church archives confirm that Pokr Khanchali’s families came from Dinarigom on the Karin plain, where, the family remembers, “Bdo’s people” were a recognised house.
  3. They moved together and stayed together.The family crossed the frontier as a group in 1829–1830 and put down roots in one village, Pokr Khanchali. There its patriarch, Bdoyents Papo, built the first mill, and eighty years later the Bdoyans still lived wall to wall as one kin quarter.
  4. The name came from Karin.Clans that moved to Javakhk kept the names they had borne in Karin before 1829. Bdoyans are few in number, and as far as is known, all trace their roots to Javakhk — and through it to the Karin plain.

So the Bdoyans are not simply people who share a name. They are one extended family, cousins of one another at greater or lesser distance, all descended in the male line from Bdo. His blood also runs in many families with other surnames, through the daughters of each generation who married into other houses.

Proving it with DNABecause a Y-chromosome passes almost unchanged from father to son, Bdoyan men from different branches should share Bdo’s Y-DNA signature. The most telling test now is to compare a man of the Abul line with one from Pokr Khanchali: a match would join the two branches scientifically and would show Bdo’s deep ancestral lineage in the Armenian Highlands.

The Bdoyans of Abul

Vard’s notes also reveal a second Bdoyan line in Javakhk. In Abul, a village 10 km east of Akhalkalaki, lived the singer and church clerk Smbat Bdoyan (1887–1963), whose grandfather Hakob Bdoyan had come to Abul in 1830 from Umudum, a Karin village 16 km north of the city — almost exactly as far out as Dinarigom. The Abul singers Ruben and Sirun Bdoyan, whom Vard recorded in Yerevan in 1965, still sang songs that had come with their family from Umudum.

The family’s account and this record point the same way: back to one small stretch of the Karin plain north of the city. A great household there could easily have had a branch in a neighbouring village before 1829, through a son who took land or a brother who married into it, and in the family’s view — that every Bdoyan descends from Bdo — Abul’s Hakob was of Bdo’s line too. This is the one link in the family story that a document or a DNA test still has to confirm, and Chapter VII shows how.

VI
Chapter VI THE FAMILY TODAY

Where the Bdoyans Live Today

Բդոյաններն այսօր

For a century after 1830 the family stayed rooted in Pokr Khanchali. Then, in the twentieth century, education, work and upheaval carried its branches outward — first to Armenia and Tbilisi, then deep into Russia, and finally overseas.

The pattern followed the paths taken by Javakhk Armenians in general. In the Soviet decades young people went to Yerevan to study and stayed for work; Vard Bdoyan, who moved there in the 1930s, is the clearest example. Others settled just across the border in Gyumri and the towns of Shirak, or in Tbilisi, the capital of their own republic. From the 1960s many Javakhk men went to work seasonally in southern Russia, and whole families followed them to Krasnodar, Sochi, Rostov, Stavropol and the spa towns. After 1991, economic collapse pushed a new wave to Moscow and to Europe and North America.

Chapter VII THE GUIDE

Finding the Exact Family Tree

Ինչպես վերականգնել տոհմածառը

The full tree from Bdo to today — the Armenian tohmatsar (տոհմածառ) — can be rebuilt. The method is to start with the living and work backwards, one generation and one record at a time.

Each period of the family’s history left a different kind of record, kept in a different place. Think of the search as five layers, from the present back to Bdo:

Step by step

  1. Begin with the eldersRecord (audio or video) the oldest Bdoyans, especially anyone born in Pokr Khanchali before about 1960. Ask for names, nicknames, the branch or “house” within the family, where each brother went, and who married whom. Copy every document and photograph, and write down who is in each picture. Where: every Bdoyan household. Share the results, because each branch remembers a different part.
  2. Read the stonesPhotograph every Bdoyan gravestone in the cemetery around St Hakob in Pokr Khanchali, and in Ninotsminda and Akhalkalaki. Note names, dates and any patronymic (“son of …”). Gravestones often fill gaps for the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Where: Pokr Khanchali church cemetery; town cemeteries of Javakhk.
  3. Search every spellingThe name appears as Բդոյան in Armenian, Бдоян in Russian and ბდოიანი (Bdoiani) in Georgian records, and as Bdoyan, Bdojan or Bdoian in Latin script. Older documents may write the clan form, “of the house of Bdo”.
    Collect civil recordsFor people born or married after the 1920s, request certified extracts of birth, marriage and death records. Close relatives can usually request them; for older entries the archive of the civil registry may need to be searched. Georgia: Public Service Development Agency (civil registry, Ministry of Justice). Armenia: Civil Status Registration Agency (ՔԿԱԳ), Ministry of Justice.
  4. Reading a registerEntries are in Armenian, later partly in Russian. A baptism gives the child’s name, the dates of birth and baptism, and both parents’ names; a marriage names the fathers of bride and groom. Each entry lets you climb one generation.
    Search the church registers (metrical books)From the 1830s until about 1920, the priests of St Hakob kept registers of baptisms, marriages and deaths. Copies went to the diocesan consistory in Tiflis. Each entry names the parents, so you can climb back a generation at a time. Start with: FamilySearch films 1915055 and 1915180 (see the box above). Then: National Archives of Armenia, Yerevan (archives.am), for the original files; and the Central Historical Archive in Tbilisi (archive.gov.ge), whose online finder lists about 400 metrical books from 177 Armenian churches in Georgia. Ask for “Pokr (Patara) Khanchali, Akhalkalaki uyezd” — Tiflis governorate from 1867, Kutais governorate before that.

    Find the first Russian listsThe household lists and descriptions drawn up in the 1830s–1880s, the family lists of 1886 and the 1897 census list every household head with his sons and their ages. The earliest of these, made soon after 1830, are the most likely documents to name Bdo’s sons or grandsons with their fathers’ names — the bridge back to Bdo himself. Its notes on storytellers (pp. 707–738) give names, birth years, parents and marriages, and pp. 148–152 describe the village house by house. Read: The Ethnography and Folk Art of the Armenians of Javakhk (YSU Press, 2014), arar.sci.am.     Autosomal (anyone) finds cousins on all sides, back about five or six generations.
    Test DNAAsk two or three Bdoyan men from different branches — one of them from the Abul line — to take a Y-DNA test (Y-37 or higher), and join the Armenian DNA Project. Matching results confirm a shared male-line ancestor. Autosomal tests (such as AncestryDNA, MyHeritage or FamilyTreeDNA Family Finder) help connect cousins through female lines. Where: FamilyTreeDNA, Armenian DNA Project (familytreedna.com).

Where: FamilySearch (free, familysearch.org), Geni or MyHeritage. On FamilySearch, also search the indexed collection Armenia, Church Books, 1838–1929 (about 94,000 names) for Բդոյան, Бдоян and Bdoyan.

Questions to ask the elders

Background on KarinFor the villages of the Karin plain before 1915, see the Houshamadyan project (houshamadyan.org), which documents the Armenian towns and villages of the Ottoman Empire. Ottoman records from before 1830 rarely name individual villagers, so the earliest Russian lists are the realistic bridge to Bdo.
  • What was your father’s name, and his father’s, as far back as you know? (The old ideal was yot port — seven generations.)
  • Which branch are we — which of the five houses in Pokr Khanchali (Harutyun’s, Grigor’s, Anton and Davit’s, Mesrop’s, Zakar’s), and who lived there with whom?
  • What is remembered of Bdo’s full name, of Bdoyents Papo and his mill, and of the Bdoyans of Abul?
  • Which brothers or cousins left, when, and where did they go?
  • Who married into the family, and from which villages and families?
  • Are there documents, letters, Bibles or photographs with names and dates written on them?

Family and maps

Family tradition, as given by the Bdoyan family for this booklet: Bdo as founder, his house in Dinarigom, and the descent of all Bdoyans from him.

Map data: Natural Earth (public domain), 1:10m and 1:50m cultural and physical vectors. Historical frontier, routes and family centres drawn for this booklet; the small lakes of Javakheti are shown schematically.

A note on certaintyConfirmed by records: Pokr Khanchali’s settlement from Dinarigom in 1829–1830; Bdoyents Papo and his mill; the five Bdoyan houses of the 1910s; the Abul line from Hakob Bdoyan of Umudum; Vard Bdoyan’s life and work; the church history; the archive references. Family tradition: Bdo himself, his house in Dinarigom, and the descent of every Bdoyan, the Abul line included, from him. Estimates: Bdo’s baptismal name (probably Petros), his dates, generation counts, Papo’s generation, Varvare as Vard’s mother, the likely centres on the maps. All can be tested against the records in Chapter VII and Appendix B.
