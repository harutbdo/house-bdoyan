Chapter I THE FOUNDER

Bdo of the Karin Plain

Բդո՝ Կարնո դաշտից

Every Armenian surname is a small history. Bdoyan — Բդոյան — is the name of one man’s household, carried for more than two centuries across two empires and three modern countries.

The nameԲդո + -յան = Բդոյան
The Armenian ending -yan (older -ean) means “of” or “belonging to the line of”. Bdoyan therefore simply means “of Bdo’s family”. In village speech the household was also called by the collective form Բդոյենց, Bdoyents — “Bdo’s people”.The form “Bdoyents” is used in Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 91, 575, 730.

The story begins on the plain of Karin — today’s Erzurum in eastern Turkey. It is a broad, almost treeless upland at close to 1,900 metres, ringed by mountains and drained by the headwaters of the Euphrates. For centuries it was the heart of Bardzr Hayk, “High Armenia”, and its fields were worked from Armenian farming villages; at the start of the nineteenth century there were roughly a hundred of them on the plain alone.Wikipedia, “Monastery of Saint Minas of Kes”, on the Armenian villages of the Erzurum plain before 1829.

Family tradition places the home of Bdo (Bedros) in one of these villages: Dinarigom (Դինարիգոմ) ○, and the records now bear out that the family came from there ●. Samvel Karapetian’s survey of Javakhk, built on the church archives, states that the people of Pokr Khanchali descend from Armenians who moved there from Dinarigom village, Karin district, in 1829–1830, and places Dinarigom on the right bank of the Euphrates, 15 km north of Karin city; its Turkish name today is Myratgeldi.Karapetian, Javakhk, 530 and note 1. The village also appears, spelled Դինարգոմ (Dinargom), in the 1909 list of the forty-eight Armenian villages of the Karin district — so the Armenians who stayed behind in 1829 went on living there for another eighty years.Soviet Armenian Encyclopedia, vol. 5, “Karin” (district).

Who was Bdo?

No document yet found carries his name, and nothing written in his own hand survives. What we can say comes from the name itself and from how Armenian village society worked.

One thing is almost certain: Bdo was not the name he was baptised with. Armenian priests christened children with the names of saints and of the Bible — Petros, Hakob, Grigor, Baghdasar — and no one was baptised “Bdo”. In daily life, though, villagers called each other by short pet forms ending in -o, and the notes Vard Bdoyan took in Pokr Khanchali are full of them: Papo, Khnko, Gevo for Gevorg.Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 683, 730. Bdo is one of these.

The likeliest full name is Petros (Peter) — in the Western Armenian pronunciation of Karin, Bedros ◐. Hrachia Acharyan’s great Dictionary of Armenian Personal Names gives Peto (Պետո) and Petik as the usual pet forms of Petros.Acharyan, Dictionary of Armenian Personal Names, vol. 4, entry “Petros”. The Karin dialect belongs to the Western Armenian family, in which Petros is pronounced Bedros — so Peto sounded like Bedo.Armenian Historical Grammar project, “Classical Armenian phonology” (Western Petros [bedros]); Armenian Wikipedia, “Karno barbar”. Vard Bdoyan’s dialect texts from Javakhk use the Western present “kə tesni”, “he sees”: Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 414. Written in the Eastern Armenian spelling used in the Caucasus, that sound becomes Բդո: Armenian script does not write the short vowel ə after a first consonant, and the name is said “Bə-do”. A Western Armenian family from Bitlis, the Bedoyans, likewise remember that their surname grew out of Bedrosian.Houshamadyan, “Bedoyan Collection — Athens and Nea Erythraia”.

Baghdasar has also been suggested, but a pet form of Baghdasar would normally keep its gh, which Bdo lacks. Only a register naming one of his sons as “son of Petros” — or of some other name — can settle it, and that is one of the first things the family is now looking for. Until then this book calls him Bdo (Bedros). Pet names like this were how neighbours actually spoke, and they often outlived the formal name.

Dating BdoVard Bdoyan was born in 1910, son of Harutyun. At about 28–32 years per generation, counting back five or six steps from Vard places Bdo’s birth somewhere around 1740–1780 ○. His sons and grandsons were the generation that made the journey of 1829–1830.

More telling is that his name, rather than his father’s or his son’s, became the family’s for good. In the villages of the Armenian Highlands a family line was usually called after the head of household whose authority and number of descendants made that house stand out, and in Javakhk each kin quarter was named after the eldest of its clan.Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 150; summarised by R. Gharibyan, ibid., 21. When the household divided, each branch still answered to that founder’s name. That Bdo became such a name-giver tells us he was the respected tanuter (տանուտեր), the master of the house, of a large and lasting family.

Life in a patriarchal household

Bdo’s family would have lived as a gerdastan (գերդաստան): married sons stayed under their father’s roof with their wives and children, so that three or four generations, often several dozen people, shared one hearth, one store of grain and one set of fields. Authority ran from the eldest man down; daughters married out into other houses, and brides came in.On the Armenian great family: Nahapetyan, “A Great Benefactor”, summarising Vard Bdoyan’s work; Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 161–186.

This is not guesswork borrowed from elsewhere. It is the way of life that Bdo’s own descendant, the ethnographer Vard Bdoyan, spent his career describing (see Chapter IV). He showed that such great households survived across the Armenian Highlands right up to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and that the villages themselves were built in clustered quarters of joined houses — the physical shape of clan loyalty and of communities that had to defend themselves.Nahapetyan, “A Great Benefactor”; Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 148–153.

A voice from DinarigomIn July 1959 Vard Bdoyan interviewed Simon Davtyan (formerly Kopalyan), a man from Dinarigom who had settled in Leninakan (Gyumri). Simon told him that his father’s household had numbered 33 people, with five cradles rocking in the house at the same time.

Dinarigom gives us a living picture of such a house. In 1959 Vard Bdoyan wrote down the memories of a man from that very village whose father’s household had held thirty-three people.Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 707 (note to the legend of the Khachapayt spring). His description of a Dinarigom house speaks of the same scale: one long building with four gomasenyak (գոմասենյակ) — the raised winter rooms built inside the cattle barn, where the family spent the cold months beside its animals.Ibid., 183, 201.

On the Karin plain that life meant spring ploughing with heavy wooden ploughs drawn by teams of oxen, wheat and barley harvested in a short summer, flocks taken to the uplands, and long winters when people and animals sheltered in the same half-sunken stone buildings for warmth.General description after Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, chapters on farming, herding and dwellings. It was a hard, close-knit and self-reliant world. In 1828 it was overtaken by war.
