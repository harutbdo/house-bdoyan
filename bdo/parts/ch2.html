The Great Exodus, 1829–1830

In the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–1829 the Russian Caucasus army under General Ivan Paskevich swept into the Ottoman borderlands. Kars fell in the summer of 1828, followed by Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe; in the summer of 1829 the army entered Erzurum itself. For the Armenians of Karin, who had welcomed and supplied the Russian troops, it seemed that Christian rule had arrived to stay.

It had not. By the Treaty of Adrianople, signed on 14 September 1829 (2 September in the Old Style calendar used in Russia), Russia kept the districts of Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe — the region Armenians call Javakhk — but handed Erzurum, Kars and Bayazet back to the Sultan. Fearing reprisals, and encouraged by the Russian authorities who wanted settlers for their new frontier lands, the Armenians of the province chose to leave with the withdrawing army.

The emigration was led by the Armenian prelate of Erzurum, Archbishop Karapet Bagratuni (1779–1856), and it used a right written into the treaty itself: Article 13 allowed the inhabitants of the returned lands to move freely to the other empire. The Ardahan villages left at the end of 1829; the people of Erzurum, Basen and Khnus followed in the summer of 1830. Whole villages packed what they could onto ox-carts — grain, tools, bedding, livestock, the vestments and books of their churches — and moved north-east in columns over the mountain roads toward Kars and the new Russian frontier. Families travelled together and, wherever they could, settled together; a village that left as one community tried to rebuild itself as one community.

For the people of Dinarigom and their neighbours, the road led to the high, half-empty plateau of Javakheti, around the lakes of Khanchali and Paravani. Decades of border warfare had left much of it thinly settled, and the Russian administration directed the newcomers there to farm it. The villages of the Karin plain scattered across that plateau side by side: Dinarigom’s families founded Pokr Khanchali; people from Umudum, which lay at almost the same distance north of the city (16 km), settled Abul and Majadia; those of Hindzk went first to Sazel near Akhaltsikhe and then to Mets Khanchali, across the lake from Pokr Khanchali.

Vard Bdoyan describes how they did it. By old custom, he writes, the Armenians resettled by forming kin quarters: “they acquired not only single houses but whole quarters”, and it was thought unacceptable for a kinsman to build apart from the rest of his clan. A family that had lived as one quarter in Karin rebuilt itself as one quarter in Javakhk.

The move was not just a change of country. Karin and Javakheti lie at almost the same height — about 1,900 metres against 1,950 — with the same short summers, deep snows and open, windswept fields. The migrants brought their ploughs, seed, customs and dialect, and much of that life could be lived again on the new plateau. That continuity is one reason the families of Javakhk kept their Karin identity so strongly, and why the Karin dialect is still spoken there today.
