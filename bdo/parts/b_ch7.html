Chapter VII THE FAMILY TODAY

Where the Bdoyans Live Today

Բդոյաններն այսօր

For a century after 1830 the family stayed rooted in two villages of Javakhk. In the twentieth century its members began to move — and today the Bdoyans are still, overwhelmingly, a family of Georgia, Armenia and Russia.

How many, and where

Surname-statistics services, which compile public registers and phone directories, estimate that about 1,440 people bear the name Bdoyan today. Some 559 of them live in Georgia, 499 in Armenia and 374 in Russia; only a handful are recorded anywhere else — two in the United States, one each in Belgium, Bulgaria and Abkhazia.surnam.es, “Bdoyan surname”. The figures are modelled estimates, not a census; they are given to show proportions only. These are estimates, not a count, but the proportions are clear: almost every Bdoyan still lives within a day’s journey of Javakhk or in the Russian cities where Javakhk families have worked for generations.

Moves the records show

Vard Bdoyan’s notes on his storytellers record where several Bdoyans went in the twentieth century. They are the first documented branches of the family outside Javakhk (Map 2):Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 707 (Gevorg), 711 (Khachatur), 720 (Balasan; Ruben and Sirun), 747 (Vard).

  • Vard left Pokr Khanchali for the college in Akhalkalaki and then for Yerevan, where he lived and worked from the late 1930s until his death in 1986.
  • Gevorg of Akhalkalaki, a shoemaker born in 1890, lived in Kharkiv (now Ukraine), in Yerevan and in the village of Chaltyr, near Rostov-on-Don in southern Russia.
  • Khachatur, born in Pokr Khanchali in 1888, lived there, in Mamzara and in Akhaltsikhe.
  • Balasan, born in Pokr Khanchali in 1930, lived in the village, in the Altai in Siberia, and in Yerevan.
  • Ruben and Sirun of Abul moved with their family to Katnatu, a new village of the Bogdanovka (Ninotsminda) district, in the 1920s, and to Yerevan in 1964.

The wider pattern

These individual moves fit the paths taken by Javakhk Armenians in general. In the Soviet decades young people went to Yerevan to study and stayed for work. Others settled just across the border in Gyumri and the towns of Shirak, or in Tbilisi, the capital of their own republic.
