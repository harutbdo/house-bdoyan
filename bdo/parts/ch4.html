The best-known Bdoyan is the ethnographer and folklorist Vard Harutyuni Bdoyan — a son of Pokr Khanchali who spent his life recording exactly the kind of village world his ancestor Bdo had lived in.

Vard Bdoyan was born on 4 March 1910 in Pokr Khanchali, into a farming family. He went to school in the village, finished the pedagogical college in Akhalkalaki in 1932 and taught in secondary schools from 1933 to 1937 before settling in Yerevan, where he completed the Pedagogical Institute by correspondence in 1943.

He never lost touch with home: in 1961 he gave some 300 objects to the regional museum of Akhalkalaki, and he argued in print that Javakhk deserved a museum of its own.

What Vard Bdoyan said

Vard Bdoyan’s ideas speak directly to the Bdoyan story. Here are six of them, drawn from his published work as summarised by the ethnographer Rafik Nahapetyan on his centenary in 2010, and from his Javakhk book.

On ancestors

“There is no people whose later art of dress is not directly connected with the art of its ancestors.”

«Չկա ժողովուրդ, որը տարազի ուշ շրջանի արվեստով անմիջականորեն կապված չլինի իր նախնիների արվեստի հետ»

His conviction that the present is always tied to the ancestors runs through all his work. He traced each region’s costume back to the ancient peoples of the Armenian Highlands and rejected the claim that Armenian dress was simply borrowed from neighbours.

On the great household
The gerdastan lasted for centuries

He traced the patriarchal great family to the rise of the father-centred clan, and showed how it survived through the feudal centuries before breaking up only in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — the very world Bdo’s family lived in.

On kinship words
Native words for “kin”

He held that the words for a blood-kin group, արյունառու (aryunaru) and ցեղ (tsegh), are native Armenian, while ազգ (azg) and տոհմ (tohm) came from Pahlavi and Persian. Through these terms he followed the links between the wider clan and the family line.

On the village
Houses built for kin and defence

The Armenian village of attached quarters and joined houses, which lasted into the 20th century, was in his view the product of two forces: clan tradition on one side and communities defending themselves under hostile rulers on the other.

On hidden memory
Old beliefs survive in disguise

His “theory of disguise” held that ancient beliefs did not vanish with Christianity but lived on in hidden forms. The female-shaped clay salt-cellars of Armenian homes, he argued, still carried the image of the old goddess Anahit, protector of mother and child.

On resettling as kin

“They acquired not only single houses, but whole quarters.”

«ձեռք էին բերում ոչ միայն առանձին տներ, այլև ամբողջական թաղեր»

Writing of the 1830s in Javakhk, he showed that the settlers from Karin rebuilt their villages clan by clan, and that a kinsman who built far from his clan’s quarter was frowned upon. Each quarter was named after the clan’s eldest — which is how “Bdo’s people” could carry his name from the Karin plain to Lake Khanchali.

The book that came out after him

For years it seemed that his most important work for the family would never appear. His archive held about 130 printer’s sheets ready for print, including two large studies of his homeland, The Ethnography of Javakhk and The Folklore of Javakhk; some 20 sheets of the folklore went to the Hayastan publishing house in 1986, the year he died, and were never printed there. Years later an editor handed the folklore manuscript — more than five hundred large pages, saved, as he puts it, by sheer chance from being burned — to the folklorist Rafayel Gharibyan, himself from Javakhk. Gharibyan traced the family and returned it; they entrusted him with the ethnography manuscript as well, and he spent years turning both into one book.

It appeared in 2014 from Yerevan State University Press, with support from the Gulbenkian Foundation: The Ethnography and Folk Art of the Armenians of Javakhk («Ջավախքի հայոց ազգագրությունը և ժողովրդական բանարվեստը»), 752 pages, compiled, introduced and annotated by Gharibyan, with drawings by Vard himself. It is the source of the village sketch, the mill of Bdoyents Papo and the Dinarigom household in this booklet. It does not mention Bdo by name: the link to Dinarigom comes from the church archives quoted by Karapetian, and the founder’s name from the family.

Vard Bdoyan began collecting at home. His very first recordings, in 1938, were made from an old storyteller and from “his relative” Gevorg Bdoyan of Akhalkalaki; in the summer of 1940 he worked in Pokr Khanchali itself, and among his best storytellers his editor names Varvare, Anush and Balasan Bdoyan. His notes on each of them, with birthplaces and dates, read like the first pages of a family register (see the box).

Varvare Bdoyan, who married Harutyun Bdoyan of Pokr Khanchali in 1887, was a skilled dairywoman and potter who knew many proverbs. Vard was “son of Harutyun” and was born in 1910, and house 28 in his sketch is Harutyun’s, so she was very probably his mother — though the notes never say so, and a register will have to confirm it. Anush lost her husband Mesrop in the fighting of 1918, his brother in 1919 and both her sons, Grigor and Zavik, in 1941–1942; she turned her grief into verses that Vard wrote down and kept with deep respect.
