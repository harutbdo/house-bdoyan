Afterword HOW IT WAS POSSIBLE

How This History Could Be Written

Ինչպես հնարավոր դարձավ այս պատմությունը

Most Armenian families cannot follow one ancestor across two centuries and two empires. The Bdoyans can, for three reasons: the kind of name they carry, the work of one of their own, and the tools that have only recently put scattered records within reach.

1 · Bdoyan began as a clan name, not a surname

Before the nineteenth century Armenian villagers had no surnames in the modern sense. A man was known by his own name, his father’s name and the name of his tohm (տոհմ), the lineage or house he belonged to. That house name, the tohmanun (տոհմանուն), was taken from a founding ancestor and passed down the male line: Bdoyents, “Bdo’s people”.On the clan (azg, tohm) and the house named after its eldest: Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 148–152; Gharibyan, preface, ibid., 21. Only under Russian rule, when officials began to write every household into registers and lists, did these house names harden into fixed surnames.See Chapter III, “The surname becomes official”.

That history is what makes the Bdoyans traceable. A common surname such as Grigoryan was formed again and again from thousands of unrelated men called Grigor. A tohmanun names one house. Everyone born a Bdoyan therefore belongs, by the family’s own rule, to the line of one man, and the name stayed attached to one kin quarter of one village long enough for the first registers to catch it there. It also explains why the name is so rare (about 1,440 people) and so concentrated in Javakhk and the places its people moved to.surnam.es, “Bdoyan surname” (modelled estimate; see Chapter VII).

A word of cautionA house could also take in a son-in-law who came to live with his wife’s family, or an adopted child, and give him its name. That is why the book marks the descent of every Bdoyan from Bdo as family tradition ○ until Y-DNA tests from several branches confirm it.

2 · The work of Vard Bdoyan

The second reason is that the family produced its own ethnographer. Vard Bdoyan spent half a century recording how the Armenians of Javakhk lived, and he began at home. His Javakhk book, published in 2014, gives what no register does: the plan of Pokr Khanchali in the 1910s with the five Bdoyan houses side by side, the story of Bdoyents Papo and his mill, the custom by which the settlers of 1830 rebuilt whole kin quarters, and a note on every storyteller he recorded, with birth years, parents, marriages and moves.Bdoyan, Ethnography and Folk Art of Javakhk, 148–152 (village plan), 730 (Papo’s mill), 707–738 (storytellers). Most of the names in Chapter VI come from those notes. Without them the family’s story would begin with the first Soviet certificates.

His work also tells researchers where to go next. Every note cites a numbered field notebook, and his notes on where each family came from point to the right villages on the Karin plain, Umudum for the Abul line among them. The book that grew out of his archive is in effect the family’s first research guide.

3 · New tools

The third reason is recent. Twenty years ago the sources for this book were scattered across reading rooms in Yerevan, Tbilisi and Istanbul. Today many of them are a search away:

  • Digital libraries. Vard Bdoyan’s 2014 book, Ashot Melkonyan’s history of Javakhk and Rafik Nahapetyan’s biography are free PDFs in the Pan-Armenian Digital Library; Samvel Karapetian’s village survey is on the RAA website; Acharyan’s dictionary of names is on the Internet Archive.
  • Archive catalogues online. The parish index of the National Archives of Armenia, the FamilySearch microfilm catalogue and the metrical-book finder of the National Archives of Georgia show exactly which registers survive for Pokr Khanchali and where.
  • Open pictures and maps. Photographs shared on Wikimedia Commons under open licences, and the public-domain map data of Natural Earth, made the illustrations possible without a photographer or a cartographer.
  • Surname statistics. Services that compile public registers give a first estimate of how many Bdoyans live where.
  • Artificial intelligence. An AI model, Claude, made by Anthropic, helped search these sources, read scanned Armenian books (including text in an old computer encoding that no longer displays correctly), translate and compare passages, draw the maps and charts, and set the pages. It does not replace the archives or the family’s memory: every fact in this book is tied to a source so that a person can check it.

None of these tools can read a register that has not yet been opened, or remember what only an elder remembers. The next chapter of this history will come, as the first one did, from the family itself.
