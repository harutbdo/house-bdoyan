Chapter III THE VILLAGE

A New Home on Lake Khanchali

Փոքր Խանչալի · Ջավախք

On the south-western shore of a shallow upland lake, the migrants from Karin founded the village that would be the Bdoyans’ home for the next two centuries: Pokr Khanchali.

Pokr Khanchali today Armenian: Փոքր Խանչալի (“Little Khanchali”)
Georgian: პატარა ხანჩალი (Patara Khanchali)
Country: Georgia, Samtskhe-Javakheti region, Ninotsminda municipality
Location: about 5.5 km south-west of Ninotsminda (formerly Bogdanovka)
Altitude: about 1,930–1,980 m
Sister village: Mets (Didi) Khanchali, on the same lake
Settled: 1829–1830, by Armenians from Dinarigom, Karin
Population: 554 (2014 census)

Pokr Khanchali — “Little Khanchali”, to distinguish it from its larger neighbour Mets Khanchali across the lake — stretches along the southern shore of Lake Khanchali, within 200 metres of the water, on the Javakheti volcanic plateau in today’s Georgia, close to the Armenian border. Its people, Samvel Karapetian writes, descend from the families who came from Dinarigom in 1829–1830, and it has been an Armenian village ever since. For a time it had a few Turkish neighbours as well: a document of 1870 counts eight houses of Tarakama Turks (96 people), there were six in 1884, and in 1918 they left for Turkey.

The Bdoyan quarter

We can see exactly where the Bdoyans lived. In 1950 Vard Bdoyan climbed the rocky hill called Kurch above the village and sketched it house by house, leaving out everything new, so as to record Pokr Khanchali as it had stood in the 1910s. The village was divided into an upper and a lower half, veri mayla and tsatsi mayla, and each half into groups of joined houses, every group the quarter of one or more related families. The old Armenian word for such a quarter, tagh, Vard notes, had already been forgotten in Karin: the settlers called it a mahla, and named it after the eldest of the clan.

The Bdoyans held one block of five adjoining houses at the end of the first group of the lower quarter, beside the Vardanyans and the Yesoyans: the houses of Harutyun, Grigor, Anton and Davit, Mesrop and Zakar Bdoyan. It is the kin quarter in its plainest form — the descendants of one house still living wall to wall eighty years after the migration.

Bdoyents Papo and the first mill

The Bdoyan hay teamHaymaking was done by teams called hampa, formed by kinship and kept almost unchanged for decades: the Ispirents, Avagents, Bdoyents, Davitents, Esoyents and Vardanents teams, among others. Each team cut the first letter of its leader’s surname into its haystacks with a spade, so that Բ 30 meant “Bdoyan, 30 haycocks”.

Vard also preserved the name of the family’s leader in the new village. In the 1830s, he writes, the first water-mill of Pokr Khanchali was built by Bdoyents Papo — “Papo of the Bdoyans” — the nahapet (նահապետ), or patriarch, of the Bdoyans of the village. Water was scarce on the slope of the hill called Atorik where he built it, so it turned only one millstone, but it went on working long after him. When it had come down to Papo’s great-grandsons, the Ayvazyans built a handier mill closer to the village, and a village song teased the Bdoyans about their old one. In 1918 raiders wrecked both mills, and the villagers had to take their grain to the mills of Dilif.

Papo is the earliest Bdoyan known by name. As head of the family in the first years after 1830, he belonged to the generation of Bdo’s sons or grandsons; his great-grandsons were most likely the men of Vard’s sketch.

Counting the village

Every year the diocese asked each parish how many souls it had, so the village can be followed almost year by year. The 78 people of 1853 had become 147, in thirteen houses, by 1866, and 691 by 1914. Then came catastrophe. In 1918 the Ottoman army overran Javakhk; the villagers fled, and hunger and disease followed the refugees. By 1921 only 353 people were left. Anush Bdoyan’s husband, Mesrop Bdoyan — very probably the owner of house 31 — fell fighting in the Dilif pass in May 1918, and his brother Khnko was killed in the village in January 1919. Pokr Khanchali recovered to 593 people by 1926 and to 801, in 215 houses, by 1987. In the Second World War it sent 113 men to the front, and 58 did not come back. The 2014 census counted 554 inhabitants, 99% of them Armenian.

The church of St Hakob

A village of Karin Armenians was organised around its church. Surb Hakob (Սուրբ Հակոբ, St James) is mentioned as early as the 1850s, and in the 1860s it was still a modest wooden building. In 1875 the villagers rebuilt it, with finely dressed stone on the west front and hewn stone on the other walls. It is a hall of 16.8 × 10.3 metres with walls 90 cm thick; inside, two pairs of round columns carry the double-pitched roof. The only door is in the west wall, and the font, cut from a single block of stone, is set into the north wall. A parish school was already working in 1865, with one teacher and ten pupils.

Two inscriptions record the rebuilding. The one on and around the tympanum over the door says, in our translation, that in the days of Catholicos Gevorg the church of Pokr Khanchali “was built and dedicated to Saint Hakob” and blessed by Archbishop Grigor Sakinyan of Akhaltsikhe; its worn last lines include the name of Father Vardan. The second, carved south of the door, says that the church was consecrated “through the efforts and the honest earnings of my dear people” on the feast of the Holy Cross of Varag, 28 September 1875.

The records also name people who served it. Father Vardan Movsisyan was one of its priests from the 1860s to the 1880s — very probably the Father Vardan of the inscription, and the man most likely to have written down the baptisms, weddings and burials of the Bdoyans in those decades. Karapet Khoperyants taught in the parish school in 1875, and at the start of the twentieth century the church held a capital of 257 roubles 69 kopeks in silver. Around the church lies a cemetery with gravestones of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the village also keeps a memorial to the fedayi commander Gevorg Chavush (1870/71–1907).

That church matters to anyone tracing the family. For almost ninety years, from the 1830s to the Soviet period, its priests wrote down every baptism, marriage and burial in the village. Those registers are the most direct written trail from today’s Bdoyans back to the families who arrived in 1830 (see Chapter VII). The gravestones around the church are a second record, carved in stone.

The surname becomes official

The district in numbers1893 — the Akhalkalaki district has 102 villages and about 60,000 people.
1897 — the first Russian census counts 72,709 people in the district; 72% give Armenian as their mother tongue.

In Karin, people were known by their own name, their father’s name and their house. Under Russian rule the authorities began to list every household, its head and his sons in registers, censuses and family lists. Over the course of the nineteenth century the old house-names hardened into fixed surnames written on every document. That is how “Bdo’s people” became, permanently and officially, the Bdoyan family.

The names themselves were older than the records. Summing up Vard Bdoyan’s findings, the editor of his Javakhk book, Rafayel Gharibyan, notes that a kin quarter was usually called after the head of the clan, and that almost every clan that moved to Javakhk kept the name it had borne in Karin before the migration. The Russian clerks wrote down names that the families had brought with them.

The settlers brought their speech with them. The Karin (Karno) dialect (Կարնո բարբառ), once spoken from Kars to Baberd with Erzurum at its centre, survives today mainly in Javakhk — in Akhalkalaki, Ninotsminda and their villages. When older people in Pokr Khanchali speak the village tongue, they are still using the words of the Karin plain that Bdo knew.

Life in Pokr Khanchali followed the rhythm the families had known in Karin: grain and potatoes on the high fields, cattle and sheep, long winters, and the gerdastan household slowly giving way, by the early twentieth century, to smaller families. Into one of those families, in 1910, was born the man who would become the Bdoyans’ best-known son.
