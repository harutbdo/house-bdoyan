How This Book Was Made

This book was put together in a single working day, 15 October 2026, by one member of the family working with an AI model. This section explains how, so that others can check the work, repeat it for another family, or take it further.

My workflow

— {{COMPILER}}

  1. Start with what the family knows. I began with our family tradition: that every Bdoyan descends from one man, Bdo, whose house stood in Dinarigom on the plain of Karin, and that our relative Vard Bdoyan had written about the family. I asked Claude for a full book: the history of Bdo, what Vard Bdoyan said, why all Bdoyans are his descendants, how to find the exact family tree, maps of where the family came from and where it lives, and a clear, readable design.
  2. Search with my own computer. I linked the work to my laptop so that Claude could use my browser to reach the libraries and archives, some of which refuse automated access. I approved each download myself: Vard Bdoyan’s 2014 book, Samvel Karapetian’s village survey and three photographs. I keep my passwords and accounts to myself; signing in to FamilySearch is my next step, not the AI’s.
  3. Ask for every reference. I asked for every source to be listed: the ones used, the ones that could still be used, and the ones that exist only on paper. That list became the research handbook.
  4. Question the evidence. I doubted that “Bdo” was a real baptismal name. The research that followed showed it is a pet name, most likely of Bedros (Petros). The book now calls him Bdo (Bedros) until a register gives his name, and I will keep looking for it.
  5. Ask for criticism. I asked for an analysis of the book’s format and for improvements based on how good family histories are made. Then I chose which suggestions to carry out: certainty marks and notes for every fact, a family chart and who’s who, larger type, proper book pages, real photographs, a new cover, and a separate handbook.
  6. Open it to the family. Finally I asked for this web version with working links and a place where every Bdoyan can contribute, and for Armenian and Russian editions, so that relatives in Armenia, Georgia and Russia can read it in their own language.

Three rules ran through the whole process: every fact is tied to a source; every statement carries a mark for how sure it is; and living people are named only where a published source already names them.

How Claude was used

What the AI did

  • Searched the web and the digital libraries, and read long PDFs page by page, including Vard Bdoyan’s 752-page book.
  • Recovered Armenian text stored in an old computer encoding (ArmSCII-8) that shows up as gibberish when copied from the PDF.
  • Translated short passages from Armenian and compared figures between sources, for example the population of Pokr Khanchali year by year.
  • Wrote the chapters, notes and index from those sources, and marked each statement as confirmed, probable or tradition.
  • Drew the maps from open map data with code, made the charts and diagrams, and designed the pages of both books and of this website.
  • Made the first Armenian and Russian translations of the book.

What it could not do

  • Sign in to accounts: FamilySearch, where the church registers are filmed, needs a person to log in.
  • Reach every site: some services blocked automated access, and the scan of Lalayan’s 1895 Javakhk was too poor to read.
  • Find Bdo’s baptismal name: no source that is online today gives it.
  • Guarantee itself. An AI can misread a scan or a word. That is why every fact has a note, why the translations should be checked by a native reader, and why the family’s corrections are welcome below.

How long would this take without AI?

A rough estimate for the same result, two illustrated books and a website in three languages, made by one person without AI. An “expert” here is a genealogist or historian who reads Armenian and Russian, knows the archives and has design help; a “non-expert” is a motivated family member starting from scratch. A working month is counted as 160 hours.
