I
Chapter I THE FOUNDER

Bdo of the Karin Plain

Բդո՝ Կարնո դաշտից

Every Armenian surname is a small history. Bdoyan — Բդոյան — is the name of one man’s household, carried for more than two centuries across two empires, three modern countries and, today, several continents.

The nameԲդո + -յան = Բդոյան
The Armenian ending -yan (older -ean) means “of” or “belonging to the line of”. Bdoyan therefore simply means “of Bdo’s family”. In village speech the household would also be called by a collective form such as Բդոենք — “Bdo’s people”.

The story begins on the plain of Karin — today’s Erzurum in eastern Turkey. It is a broad, almost treeless upland at close to 1,900 metres, ringed by mountains and drained by the headwaters of the Euphrates. For centuries it was the heart of Bardzr Hayk, “High Armenia”, and its fields were worked from Armenian farming villages. At the start of the nineteenth century there were roughly a hundred of them on the plain alone.

Family tradition places Bdo’s home in one of these villages: Dinarigom (Դինարիգոմ). The village is real. It still appears, spelled Դինարգոմ (Dinargom), in the 1909 list of the forty-eight Armenian villages of the Karin district printed in the Soviet Armenian Encyclopedia — evidence that Armenians who did not leave in 1829 went on living there for another eighty years.

Who was Bdo?

No document yet found carries Bdo’s name, and nothing written in his own hand survives. What we can say comes from the name itself and from how Armenian village society worked.

Bdo is a short, affectionate form of a fuller baptismal name. Family memory sometimes links it to Baghdasar or to Petros (Bedros); only a church register naming one of his sons as “son of …” could settle which. Pet forms like this were how neighbours actually spoke, and they often outlived the formal name.

Dating BdoVard Bdoyan was born in 1910, son of Harutyun. At about 28–32 years per generation, counting back five or six steps from Vard places Bdo’s birth somewhere around 1740–1780. His sons and grandsons were the generation that made the journey of 1829–1830.

More telling is that his name, rather than his father’s or his son’s, became the family’s for good. In the villages of the Armenian Highlands a family line was usually called after the head of household whose authority and number of descendants made that house stand out. When the household divided, each branch still answered to that founder’s name. That Bdo became such a name-giver tells us he was the respected tanuter (տանուտեր), the master of the house, of a large and lasting family.

Bdo’s family would have lived as a gerdastan (գերդաստան): married sons stayed under their father’s roof with their wives and children, so that three or four generations, often several dozen people, shared one hearth, one store of grain and one set of fields. Authority ran from the eldest man down; daughters married out into other houses, and brides came in.

This is not guesswork borrowed from elsewhere. It is the way of life that Bdo’s own descendant, the ethnographer Vard Bdoyan, spent his career describing (see Chapter IV). He showed that such great households survived across the Armenian Highlands right up to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and that the villages themselves were built in clustered quarters of joined houses — the physical shape of clan loyalty and of communities that had to defend themselves.

On the Karin plain that life meant spring ploughing with heavy wooden ploughs drawn by teams of oxen, wheat and barley harvested in a short summer, flocks taken to the uplands, and long winters when people and animals sheltered in the same half-sunken stone buildings for warmth. It was a hard, close-knit and self-reliant world. In 1828 it was overtaken by war.

Chapter II THE JOURNEY

The Great Exodus, 1829–1830

Կարնեցիների մեծ գաղթը

Between autumn 1829 and 1830, tens of thousands of Armenians left the Karin plain behind. Among them were Bdo’s sons and grandsons.

In numbers 75,000–90,000
Armenians estimated to have left Erzurum province with the Russian army, most of them from Karin itself.

35,000–40,000
of them settled in Javakhk and the neighbouring districts of Akhaltsikhe and Tsalka.

≈ 50
Armenian villages left on the Karin plain by the end of the 19th century, against about 100 at its start.

In the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–1829 the Russian Caucasus army under General Ivan Paskevich swept into the Ottoman borderlands. Kars fell in the summer of 1828, followed by Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe; in the summer of 1829 the army entered Erzurum itself. For the Armenians of Karin, who had welcomed and supplied the Russian troops, it seemed that Christian rule had arrived to stay.

It had not. By the Treaty of Adrianople, signed on 14 September 1829, Russia kept the districts of Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe — the region Armenians call Javakhk — but handed Erzurum, Kars and Bayazet back to the Sultan. Fearing reprisals, and encouraged by the Russian authorities who wanted settlers for their new frontier lands, the Armenians of the province chose to leave with the withdrawing army.

The emigration was led by the Armenian prelate of Erzurum, Archbishop Karapet Bagratuni. Whole villages packed what they could onto ox-carts — grain, tools, bedding, livestock, the vestments and books of their churches — and moved north-east in columns over the mountain roads toward Kars and the new Russian frontier. Families travelled together and, wherever they could, settled together; a village that left as one community tried to rebuild itself as one community.

For the people of Dinarigom and their neighbours, the road led to the high, half-empty plateau of Javakheti, around the lakes of Khanchali and Paravani. Decades of border warfare had left much of it thinly settled, and the Russian administration directed the newcomers there to farm it.

The move was not just a change of country. Karin and Javakheti lie at almost the same height — about 1,900 metres against 1,950 — with the same short summers, deep snows and open, windswept fields. The migrants brought their ploughs, seed, customs and dialect, and much of that life could be lived again on the new plateau. That continuity is one reason the families of Javakhk kept their Karin identity so strongly, and why the Karin dialect is still spoken there today.

Chapter III THE VILLAGE

A New Home on Lake Khanchali

Փոքր Խանչալի · Ջավախք

On the south-western shore of a shallow upland lake, the migrants from Karin founded the village that would be the Bdoyans’ home for the next two centuries: Pokr Khanchali.

Pokr Khanchali today Armenian: Փոքր Խանչալի (“Little Khanchali”)
Georgian: პატარა ხანჩალი (Patara Khanchali)
Country: Georgia, Samtskhe-Javakheti region, Ninotsminda municipality
Location: about 5.5 km south-west of Ninotsminda (formerly Bogdanovka)
Altitude: about 1,930–1,980 m
Sister village: Mets (Didi) Khanchali, on the same lake
Settled: 1829–1830, by Armenians from Karin

Pokr Khanchali — “Little Khanchali”, to distinguish it from its larger neighbour Mets Khanchali across the lake — lies on the Javakheti volcanic plateau in today’s Georgia, close to the Armenian border. Pokr Khanchali was settled in 1829–1830 by families from the Karin area, as were most Armenian villages around it, and it has remained an Armenian village ever since. Within the village, the Bdoyans are remembered as one of the founding families.

The church of St Hakob

A village of Karin Armenians was organised around its church. The parish of Pokr Khanchali is already recorded in the 1840 bulletin of the Armenian diocese of Georgia and Imereti. In the 1860s its church was still a modest wooden building; in 1875 the villagers rebuilt it in hewn stone as the church of Surb Hakob (Սուրբ Հակոբ, St James), and that September it was consecrated by Archbishop Grigor Sakinyants. A parish school had opened in 1864, funded by the community. The village cemetery lies around the church.

That church matters to anyone tracing the family. For almost ninety years, from the 1830s to the Soviet period, its priests wrote down every baptism, marriage and burial in the village. Those registers are the most direct written trail from today’s Bdoyans back to the families who arrived in 1830 (see Chapter VII). The gravestones around the church are a second record, carved in stone.

The surname becomes official

In Karin, people were known by their own name, their father’s name and their house. Under Russian rule the authorities began to list every household, its head and his sons in registers, censuses and family lists. Over the course of the nineteenth century the old house-names hardened into fixed surnames written on every document. That is how “Bdo’s people” became, permanently and officially, the Bdoyan family.

The settlers brought their speech with them. The Karin (Karno) dialect (Կարնո բարբառ), once spoken from Kars to Baberd with Erzurum at its centre, survives today mainly in Javakhk — in Akhalkalaki, Ninotsminda and their villages. When older people in Pokr Khanchali speak the village tongue, they are still using the words of the Karin plain that Bdo knew.

Life in Pokr Khanchali followed the rhythm the families had known in Karin: grain and potatoes on the high fields, cattle and sheep, long winters, and the gerdastan household slowly giving way, by the early twentieth century, to smaller families. Into one of those families, in 1910, was born the man who would become the Bdoyans’ best-known son.

The best-known Bdoyan is the ethnographer and folklorist Vard Harutyuni Bdoyan — a son of Pokr Khanchali who spent his life recording exactly the kind of village world his ancestor Bdo had lived in.

Major works
  1. The Cult of Land and Child Fertility among Armenians — thesis 1948, part published 1950
  2. Armenian Folk Games, three-volume collection and study (1963)
  3. Agricultural Culture in Armenia (1972), with a German summary in Hungary the same year
  4. Armenian Ethnography: A Brief Outline (1974) — for over thirty years the only university textbook of Armenian ethnography
  5. Armenian Salt-Cellars (1986)

Vard Bdoyan was born on 4 March 1910 in Pokr Khanchali, into a farming family. He went to school in the village, finished the pedagogical college in Akhalkalaki in 1932 and taught in secondary schools from 1933 to 1937 before settling in Yerevan, where he completed the Pedagogical Institute by correspondence in 1943.

He never lost touch with home: in 1961 he gave some 300 objects to the regional museum of Akhalkalaki, and he argued in print that Javakhk deserved a museum of its own.

What Vard Bdoyan said

Vard Bdoyan’s ideas speak directly to the Bdoyan story. Here are five of them, drawn from his published work as summarised by the ethnographer Rafik Nahapetyan on his centenary in 2010.

On ancestors

“There is no people whose later art of dress is not directly connected with the art of its ancestors.”

«Չկա ժողովուրդ, որը տարազի ուշ շրջանի արվեստով անմիջականորեն կապված չլինի իր նախնիների արվեստի հետ»

His conviction that the present is always tied to the ancestors runs through all his work. He traced each region’s costume back to the ancient peoples of the Armenian Highlands and rejected the claim that Armenian dress was simply borrowed from neighbours.

On the great household
The gerdastan lasted for centuries

He traced the patriarchal great family to the rise of the father-centred clan, and showed how it survived through the feudal centuries before breaking up only in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — the very world Bdo’s family lived in.

On kinship words
Native words for “kin”

He held that the words for a blood-kin group, արյունառու (aryunaru) and ցեղ (tsegh), are native Armenian, while ազգ (azg) and տոհմ (tohm) came from Pahlavi and Persian. Through these terms he followed the links between the wider clan and the family line.

On the village
Houses built for kin and defence

The Armenian village of attached quarters and joined houses, which lasted into the 20th century, was in his view the product of two forces: clan tradition on one side and communities defending themselves under hostile rulers on the other.

On hidden memory
Old beliefs survive in disguise

His “theory of disguise” held that ancient beliefs did not vanish with Christianity but lived on in hidden forms. The female-shaped clay salt-cellars of Armenian homes, he argued, still carried the image of the old goddess Anahit, protector of mother and child.

The chapter he did not publish

His most important work for the family may be the work that never appeared. His archive held about 130 printer’s sheets ready for print, including two large studies of his homeland, The Ethnography of Javakhk and The Folklore of Javakhk («Ջավախքի ազգագրությունը», «Ջավախքի բանահյուսությունը»). Some 20 sheets of the folklore went to the Hayastan publishing house in 1986, in his lifetime, but were still unpublished in 2010. The sources readable today do not quote him on Bdo by name; if he wrote down who founded Pokr Khanchali and where they came from, it is in these manuscripts. Finding them is a key step in Chapter VII.

Chapter V THE BLOODLINE

One Ancestor, One Family

Մեկ նախահայր, մեկ ընտանիք
THE CORE OF THE FAMILY STORY

Every Bdoyan is a descendant of Bdo. The name itself is the record: Bdoyan means “of Bdo”.

Բոլոր Բդոյանները Բդոյի ժառանգներն են։

Common surnames like Grigoryan or Hakobyan arose independently in hundreds of places, from hundreds of unrelated men named Grigor or Hakob. Bdoyan is different.

  1. The name points to one man.A surname built on a nickname like Bdo, rather than on a common saint’s name, belongs to a particular person. Bdoyan is the name of one household, not a common pattern.
  2. One village, one house.It grew out of a single gerdastan in Dinarigom on the Karin plain, where “Bdo’s people” were a recognised house.
  3. They moved together and stayed together.The family crossed the frontier as a group in 1829–1830 and put down roots in one village, Pokr Khanchali, where the name became fixed in Russian records.
  4. The name is rare.Bdoyans are few in number, and as far as the family knows, almost all trace their roots to Javakhk or to people who left it.

So the Bdoyans are not simply people who share a name. They are one extended family, cousins of one another at greater or lesser distance, all descended in the male line from Bdo. His blood also runs in many families with other surnames, through the daughters of each generation who married into other houses.

Proving it with DNABecause a Y-chromosome passes almost unchanged from father to son, Bdoyan men from different branches should share Bdo’s Y-DNA signature. Testing two or three men from distant branches would confirm the family’s single origin scientifically and would show Bdo’s deep ancestral lineage in the Armenian Highlands.

VI
Chapter VI THE FAMILY TODAY

Where the Bdoyans Live Today

Բդոյաններն այսօր

For a century after 1830 the family stayed rooted in Pokr Khanchali. Then, in the twentieth century, education, work and upheaval carried its branches outward — first to Armenia and Tbilisi, then deep into Russia, and finally overseas.

The pattern followed the paths taken by Javakhk Armenians in general. In the Soviet decades young people went to Yerevan to study and stayed for work; Vard Bdoyan, who moved there in the 1930s, is the clearest example. Others settled just across the border in Gyumri and the towns of Shirak, or in Tbilisi, the capital of their own republic. From the 1960s many Javakhk men went to work seasonally in southern Russia, and whole families followed them to Krasnodar, Sochi, Rostov, Stavropol and the spa towns. After 1991, economic collapse pushed a new wave to Moscow and to Europe and North America.

Chapter VII THE GUIDE

Finding the Exact Family Tree

Ինչպես վերականգնել տոհմածառը

The full tree from Bdo to today — the Armenian tohmatsar (տոհմածառ) — can be rebuilt. The method is to start with the living and work backwards, one generation and one record at a time.

Each period of the family’s history left a different kind of record, kept in a different place. Think of the search as five layers, from the present back to Bdo:

Today → 1930s

Elders, family papers, photos, letters, gravestones. Quickest and most important.

Before 1830
Karin

The hardest layer. Few Ottoman records name villagers.

Step by step

  1. Begin with the eldersRecord (audio or video) the oldest Bdoyans, especially anyone born in Pokr Khanchali before about 1960. Ask for names, nicknames, the branch or “house” within the family, where each brother went, and who married whom. Copy every document and photograph, and write down who is in each picture. Where: every Bdoyan household. Share the results, because each branch remembers a different part.

Questions to ask the elders

Background on KarinFor the villages of the Karin plain before 1915, see the Houshamadyan project (houshamadyan.org), which documents the Armenian towns and villages of the Ottoman Empire. Ottoman records from before 1830 rarely name individual villagers, so the earliest Russian lists are the realistic bridge to Bdo.
  • What was your father’s name, and his father’s, as far back as you know? (The old ideal was yot port — seven generations.)
  • Which “house” or branch of the Bdoyans do we belong to, and what was it called?
  • Which house in Pokr Khanchali was ours, and who lived there with whom?
  • Did anyone say what Bdo’s full name was, or what Vard Bdoyan said about him?
  • Which brothers or cousins left, when, and where did they go?
  • Who married into the family, and from which villages and families?

A note on certaintyThis booklet separates what records confirm from what the family remembers. Confirmed: Vard Bdoyan’s birth in Pokr Khanchali in 1910 and his career; the settlement of Pokr Khanchali by Karin Armenians in 1829–1830; the history of its church; the existence of Dinarigom on the Karin plain. Family tradition: Bdo’s home in Dinarigom and the details of his life. Estimates: Bdo’s dates, generation counts, and the likely centres on the maps. Each of these can be tested against the records listed in Chapter VII, and the booklet can be updated as names are found.
